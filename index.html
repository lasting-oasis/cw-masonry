Built right

        
Anatomy of a retaining wall

        
Most of what makes a wall last is behind it and under it. This is what goes into every one we build.

      

      

        
        
    
          
  1. Cap or copingSheds water off the top of the wall so it never soaks down through the courses.
    2. 
          
  2. Block wall in running bondCores reinforced with rebar and grout where the height calls for it.
    3. 
          
  3. GeogridLayers of mesh tied into the backfill that anchor the wall against soil pressure.
    4. 
          
  4. Gravel backfill and filter fabricWater drains straight down instead of building up and pushing on the wall. The fabric keeps soil from clogging the gravel.
    5. 
          
  5. Perforated drain pipeCollects water at the base and carries it to daylight.
    6. 
          
  6. Weep holesLet water through the face so pressure never builds behind it.
    7. 
          
  7. Footing on a compacted basePoured below frost depth, so the wall does not heave in winter.
    8. 
        

      

    

  


  

    

      

        
Bond patterns

        
We work in clay brick, concrete block, and both natural and manufactured stone. The bond is how the units are arranged; it changes the look and, in a structural wall, the strength.

        

          
Running bondEach course offset by half a brick. The standard for walls and the strongest common pattern.

          
Stack bondUnits aligned in straight columns for a modern look. Needs reinforcement in a load-bearing wall.

          
Flemish bondHeaders and stretchers alternate in every course. Used for historic matches and thick walls.

          
English bondAlternating courses of stretchers and headers. The strongest traditional bond for solid walls.

          
HerringbonePavers set at 45 degrees. Locks together under traffic, so it suits driveways and patios.

          
BasketweavePairs of pavers alternating direction. A calmer paving pattern for patios and paths.

        

      

      

        
Mortar, matched to the job

        
Mortar is chosen by where the masonry sits and what it carries, not by habit. Using the wrong type is a common cause of early cracking.

        
    
          
  • Type N for most above-grade walls, veneer, and chimneys.
    • 
          
  • Type S for below-grade work, foundations, and retaining walls that need higher strength.
    • 
          
  • Type M for heavy loads and work in contact with the ground.
    • 
          
  • Lime-based mortar for repointing older, softer brick, so the mortar wears before the brick does.
    •

How the mortar is tooled once it firms up. Concave and V joints shed water best; raked joints suit sheltered walls and give a shadowed look.

Questions
About masonry work

      
How long before I can use a new wall, patio, or steps?
Mortar sets within a day but keeps gaining strength for about four weeks. Light use is fine after a few days; we will tell you when it is safe to load a retaining wall, put furniture on a patio, or backfill against new work.

      
Can you match the existing brick on a repair or addition?
Usually, yes. We match brick size, color range, and texture from samples, and we match the mortar color and joint profile, which is what most people notice. Older brick that is no longer made can often be sourced as salvage.

      
Does a retaining wall need a permit?
Often, once it passes a certain height (commonly around 4 feet) or supports a load such as a driveway. Rules vary by county and city. We check before quoting and tell you what applies to your wall.

      
Why do some walls crack or bulge after a few years?
Almost always water or a weak base: no drainage behind a retaining wall, a footing that was too shallow, or soil that was never compacted. That is why our process puts footings, drainage, and reinforcement ahead of the first course of brick.

Protect what stays

        
How we keep the trees you want

        
A tree is killed more often by crushed roots than by a saw. The fence goes up before the first machine arrives.

      

      

        
        
    
          
  1. DriplineThe edge of the canopy. It is the minimum edge of the protection zone, not the whole of it.
    2. 
          
  2. Roots reach well past the driplineMost feeder roots sit in the top 18 inches of soil, where a single pass of a loaded machine crushes them.
    3. 
          
  3. Fence set beyond the driplinePosted and fenced before equipment arrives, farther out for large or old trees.
    4. 
          
  4. Machines and stockpiles stay outsideNo parking, no debris piles, no trenching inside the fence, for the whole job.

Questions
About land clearing

      
Do I need a permit to clear my own land?
Sometimes. Many counties require a land-disturbance permit above a certain area, and some cities have tree ordinances that protect larger trees. Stream and wetland buffers have their own rules. We check what applies to your parcel before we quote.

      
What happens to the stumps?
It depends on what the land is for. In a lawn or pasture, grinding them below grade is enough. Under a building pad, driveway, or septic field, the whole stump and its roots come out, because buried wood rots and the ground above it settles.

      
Can you save specific trees?
Yes. We flag them on the walk, fence off the root zone, and keep equipment and stockpiles off it. Roots extend well past the canopy, so the protection zone is larger than most people expect.

      
How long does clearing take?
A typical building lot takes a day or two. Acreage with large trees and haul-off takes longer. The estimate includes a schedule for your site.

Water goes where it should

        
Grading that keeps a crawlspace dry

        
Most wet crawlspaces and basements are a grading problem, not a waterproofing problem. This is the shape we grade toward.

      

      

        
        
    
          
  1. Grade slopes away from the houseAt least 6 inches of drop in the first 10 feet, the common building-code minimum.
    2. 
          
  2. SwaleA shallow grassed channel that carries runoff along the lot instead of letting it pool.
    3. 
          
  3. Downspouts discharge away from the wallOnto a splash block or extension, never straight into the backfill.
    4. 
          
  4. Backfill compacted in liftsLoose backfill settles for years and tilts the grade back toward the house. Compacted backfill stays put.
    5. 
          
  5. Topsoil and lawn on top onlySpread over the finished grade, never used as fill, so the slope holds its shape.
    6. 
        

      

    

  


  

    

      

        
Rough grading and finish grading

        
Most jobs need both, and they are different passes with different equipment.

        
        

          
Rough grading
The bulk earthwork. Dozers and excavators move the soil to within a few inches of the plan, and fill goes in and gets compacted. This is where drainage structures and pads take shape.

          
Finish grading
The precision pass. The surface is brought to final elevation with a grader, box blade, or skid steer and checked with a laser or GPS, then topsoil goes on where there will be lawn.

        

      

      

        
What good grading does

        
    
          
  • Slope away from the house. Building codes commonly call for at least 6 inches of fall in the first 10 feet from a foundation. Most wet crawlspaces fail this test.
    • 
          
  • Compaction in lifts. Fill placed in thin layers and compacted each time will not settle. Fill dumped in one deep pile will, taking the slab or driveway above it along.
    • 
          
  • Topsoil handled separately. Topsoil is stripped and saved, never buried under a pad, because it is too soft and organic to build on.
    • 
          
  • Erosion control from day one. Silt fence goes in before the first cut and stays until the ground is stable.
    •

Questions
About grading and drainage

      
Why does water pool in my yard or run toward the house?
Usually because the ground was never pitched away from the foundation, or because fill settled over the years and created low spots. Regrading the yard, and sometimes adding a swale or French drain, fixes the cause rather than the symptom.

      
Can you fix drainage without regrading the whole yard?
Often. A swale, a French drain, or a catch basin with a buried pipe can solve one wet area without disturbing the rest. We look at where the water comes from before recommending anything.

      
Do you bring in fill dirt, and what kind?
Yes, when the site needs more soil than it has. Under pads and driveways we use structural fill or crusher run that compacts well, and we keep topsoil for the surface only. The estimate shows the quantities.

      
Will my building pad need a compaction test?
Many building departments and engineers require one on fill pads. We compact in lifts to make it pass, and we can arrange the testing if your plans call for it.
